## Why?

Back then I used to dualboot my older laptop which has HDD.
You'd know how terrible Windows is with HDD. Everything takes
literal ages to load.

At that time I used btrfs 'cuz that's Fedora's default. After
some research (not sure if a bit of Googling counts as research lol)
on how I can make load time of apps faster, I concluded switching
to ext4 seems to be the best idea. And actually it was since every
app actually do load faster.

In fact, the way btrfs work means it causes fragmentation, just like
Windows' ntfs. In contrast ext4 doesn't as long as enough space
is available. I can defrag with `btrfs defrag` or mounting it with
`autodefrag` option in fstab, but eh, I'd rather use ext4 considering
btrfs' performance penalty is also noticeable in HDD (not so in SSD).
I ain't a filesystem expert so you guys can Google
[why this is the case (click me and I'll google for you)](https://www.google.com/search?q=why+does+btrfs+causes+fragmentation+on+hdd+but+on+ext4+it+is+minimal)

## Fast-forward to my current laptop...

It has NVMe SSD, so
you might be asking why not use btrfs on this new laptop? Well, it's
more of an inertia, since I'm used to ext4. So naturally on fedora
installer I chose ext4 instead of the default btrfs without thinking
and then forget about this filesystem shenanigans.

Then one day I found out Google switched from ext4 to f2fs for
userdata partition on Android for devices' launched with Android 11.
And since Android phones uses what PC's SSD uses (that is, NAND flash),
I naturally thought it was better for my NVMe SSD, so I switched my
partition from ext4 to f2fs. Turns out it was both right and wrong.

**Why it was right?**

1. It's faster for NVMe devices
1. Better wear levelling (though nowadays NVMe controllers do an
   excellent job on this, so this is almost redundant. Useful on
   Android devices because they're usually bare NAND flash)

**Why it was wrong?**

1. f2fs is known to have higher corruption rate when there's
   power loss. The `fsck.f2fs` tool is also not as mature as ext4.
   It's been reported to sometimes cause more corruption (kinda
   scary if you ask me tbh)

And yeah these reasoning came after I suddenly got interested again
in this topic, not when I switched to f2fs. Which is also when I
discovered xfs. Out of fear of data corruption due to data loss,
I switched to xfs. Although realistically for laptop users, power
loss doesn't really matter unlike desktop user. But still, better
safe than sorry. Recent benchmarks have also shown that xfs and ext4
[is the fastest](https://www.phoronix.com/review/linux-70-filesystems/2).
It doesn't really matter outside of high throughput
application like servers, but there's no tradeoffs (in my opinion)
compared to other FSes so why not?

<br/>
<sup>
> Well to be pedantic, btrfs has snapshotting capability so that's
a tradeoff, although I never needed to use it before, so I told myself
that I don't need it.
> 
> Furthermore according to benchmarks, btrfs performed the slowest
compared to ext4, f2fs and xfs. BUT BUT BUT don't let this stop you
from using btrfs, for average user it does not matter and is not
noticeable (well unless you use device that has HDD, especially low
RPM ones), so you should give btrfs a go if you like being able to
snapshot.
</sup>
