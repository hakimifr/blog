The first thing you notice walking up Jalan Reko is the smell, and it is not
the smell you expect. There is a self-service launderette on the left of the
shophouse row and a tyre shop on the right that pulls down its shutters at six,
so the air is roughly one part fabric softener, one part rubber, and then under
all of it, lemongrass and something charred. It sounds like a bad combination
on paper. Standing there at 8.40pm with the awning dripping on your shoulder
because it rained at four and the canvas still has not finished letting go, it
works.

Warung Tomyam Rimba occupies the back half of lot 7 and spills out onto the
five foot way, which gives you eight tables in total, three of them under the
awning. One of those three is permanently damp at the seam and everybody who
eats here regularly knows to avoid it. There used to be a different business in
this lot, some kind of mobile phone accessories place, and the old banner is
still bolted up there under the new one, which is the most Malaysian thing
about the whole building. The current banner is laminated, red lettering on
white, and lists a phone number that nobody calls because you either walk in or
you do not.

## The whiteboard and the people behind it

The menu is a whiteboard, wiped and rewritten through the night as things run
out. On the night I went, the sambal petai had been crossed out by eight and
the kerabu mangga lasted until about nine thirty, which tells you roughly what
the crowd here values.

The stall has been going for eleven years. Pak Ngah ran it alone for the first
eight as a straightforward nasi campur and tom yam operation, and the menu has
barely moved since. His wife does the kerabu and the acar at home in the
afternoon and drops them off in a cooler bag around six. His brother in law
mans the grill at the front, which is a two burner charcoal setup he is
fiercely protective of. Then there is Izzah, who came back three years ago with
a food technology degree and has been quietly changing the back of the house
ever since.

If you ask Izzah what the place is, she will say "resepi kampung, dapur yang
disesuaikan" and then look slightly embarrassed about how much she likes saying
it. It is the most accurate description of the restaurant that exists. The
recipe is old and the kitchen is new, and the second part exists entirely to
protect the first part. Nobody here is trying to reinvent tom yam. They are
trying to make sure the bowl on a slow Tuesday is the same bowl you got on a
packed Saturday in March.

## The bowl

The Tomyam Campur Rimba is RM 12.50 for one and RM 32 for the sharing pot that
will comfortably sit three people and feed four if they are students. Prawns,
squid rings, sliced chicken, straw mushrooms, a decent handful of bunga kantan,
and a broth that is clear and properly sour rather than the sweet coconut style
that has taken over a lot of places in the Klang Valley. The sourness comes
from tamarind and lime rather than a squeeze bottle, and there is a roasted
depth underneath it that I could not place until Izzah explained it.

It is not a shy bowl. Ask for pedas and they will take you at your word, and
the chilli padi in soy sauce on each table is there for people who made a
mistake.

Around the signature, the rest holds up better than it needs to. Tomyam ayam at
RM 9.50 comes in a portion that is frankly irresponsible at that price, and it
is the reason half of UKM seems to eat here. Kerabu mangga at RM 6.00 is
properly sharp instead of sweet. Telur dadar is RM 4.50 and fried thin, crisp
at the edges and folded over rather than rolled. Rice is RM 1.50, air sirap
limau is RM 3.50, and the drinks come out of one of those big glass dispensers
with the tap that always drips.

One thing worth saying out loud, because very few places at this price bother.
Prawns and squid run through most of the menu, the acar timun at RM 3.00 has
crushed peanut in it, and the broth itself is built on fish sauce, so anchovy
is in there whether or not you ordered anything that sounds like seafood. There
is a small allergen note printed on the back of the menu, and a laminated copy
taped next to the counter where the payment happens. Izzah put it there after a
customer had a reaction two years ago. She says it was mild, and she says it in
the tone of somebody who has not stopped thinking about it.

## What happens behind the counter

The wok station is the first thing that looks wrong. No gas, no blue flame
roar. It is a flat bottomed induction wok on a commercial burner with a
controller on the wall, which means the temperature is set rather than coaxed.
The rempah sits at 165°C while Pak Ngah fries it in small batches, hot enough
to bloom the chilli and the belacan without catching either of them. He was
against the whole idea for about two weeks. Now he will not go back, mostly
because a kitchen running on induction is a kitchen you can stand in for four
hours without your shirt sticking to you, and at fifty eight years old he has
decided he has earned that.

The induction conversion was not free. The shophouse wiring was never built for
it, and the first week they kept tripping the whole lot, launderette included,
until an electrician ran a dedicated line from the back. Izzah describes that
month as the worst of her life and Pak Ngah describes it as "biasa lah".

The second piece of equipment is a blast chiller, bought second hand from a
hotel in KL that was renovating. The kitchen makes the broth in batches of
about forty litres and holds it at 90°C straight through service. Whatever is
left at closing gets pulled down from 90°C to 3°C in around seventy five
minutes. Izzah explains this by talking about the temperature danger zone, the
band between 5°C and 60°C where bacteria do their best work, and she explains
it in the apologetic way people do when they assume you will find the subject
boring. A pot left on a bench overnight crosses that band slowly. A blast
chiller crosses it in just over an hour.

The chicken goes into a small immersion circulator at 68°C for about ninety
minutes and then hits the charcoal for colour. That circulator was bought for
her final year project and never went back to the lab. She still probes every
piece to 75°C before it leaves the pass, even though the sous vide step already
makes that mathematically unnecessary. Pak Ngah insists on it. His reasoning is
that the previous generation judged everything by feel and he is not going to
be the one who breaks the chain, which is a strange argument from a man who now
fries rempah on a PID controller, but nobody has bothered to point that out.

The bit that genuinely surprised me is the cheapest thing in the kitchen. Two
small sensors, one in the blast chiller and one in the walk in, take a reading
every thirty seconds and push it to a cloud dashboard, and if anything drifts
Izzah gets a phone notification. They cost a couple of hundred ringgit each.
She bought them after a KKM inspection where the officer asked to see
temperature records and there were none to show, which is a completely normal
situation for a stall like this and also completely indefensible when you write
it down. The stall displays a grade B on the wall. Izzah is openly annoyed
about the B.

## The two things that bother me

The back prep counter opens directly onto the service alley, and that is where
the trouble lives. Raw seafood is cleaned and portioned on the same unbroken
stainless steel run where the ready to eat stuff gets laid out, the fried
shallots, the cut lime, the chilli in soy. There is a plastic divider between
the two zones and nothing else. Nobody has got sick as far as anyone knows, and
the staff are visibly careful, but careful is a human quality and humans get
sloppy at 8.30pm on a Friday when there are eleven tickets on the rail. Three
colour coded chopping boards, red for raw seafood, green for vegetables, white
for ready to eat, would cost about thirty ringgit and take the risk off the
  table. Moving the condiments to the back room near the chest freezer would
  cost nothing at all, because that corner is already empty.

The second problem is quieter and probably more annoying for the people who run
the place. The cloud loggers need the internet, and the line drops out roughly
once a fortnight. When it goes, the sensors keep logging locally but the alerts
do not fire, so a chiller failing at 3am is a chiller nobody hears about until
  opening. An alarm that beeps in the kitchen regardless of connectivity would
  close that gap, and for a stall that shuts at one in the morning, that
  matters more than any dashboard.

## Service and the queue that used to ruin it

Two years ago, ordering here was a shouted conversation across the wok station
and a handwritten slip stuck on a bamboo stick. On a good night it worked fine.
On a bad night you waited three quarters of an hour, watched two tables who
arrived after you get served first, and eventually received a tomyam ayam when
you had ordered seafood. In a kitchen where every bowl is made to order, a
wrong bowl is not a small error, it is fifteen minutes of somebody's life
thrown away.

The fix is unglamorous. There is a QR code taped to each table that opens a
plain ordering page, and a kitchen display screen mounted above the pass so
tickets show up in sequence and the kitchen can see what is pending. The
average wait during the seven to nine crush is now around eighteen minutes. It
does not make the food taste better and it does not make the place feel more
modern, and honestly that is the correct outcome. The regulars still flag down
a waiter out of pure habit and nobody stops them.

## The green side, which is small and unglamorous

Used cooking oil goes to a licensed collector at around eighty sen a kilogram,
which is not real money but keeps it out of the drain. There is no polystyrene
anywhere on the premises and takeaway comes in paper lined boxes. Bring your
own container and you get a ringgit off, which is a discount about four people
a night actually claim. The kangkung comes from a neighbour's aquaponics setup
two streets over, which is less a supply chain than a favour between friends,
but it is picked the same afternoon and it travels four hundred metres.

## Verdict

Warung Tomyam Rimba is a taste driven kitchen that happens to be well
instrumented, and the instrumentation is there because somebody in the family
went to university and came back unwilling to guess. The broth is the reason to
come. The induction wok is the reason it tastes the same every time. The
loggers are the reason you can stop worrying about the chiller.

The gaps are real though, and both of them are fixable for less than the price
of a sharing pot.

4.4 out of 5. Taste 4.7, value 4.6, cleanliness 4.0, service 4.3, technology
4.5. Dinner for two with drinks came to RM 28.50. Would I go back? I already
have, twice.
